## A Practical Consulting Problem-Solving Framework

When MNP gives you an ambiguous case, do not jump directly to a solution.

1. **Clarify the objective:** What business outcome matters—cost, revenue, quality, speed, working capital, service, capacity, or risk?
2. **Define scope:** Which process, location, product, customer group, time period, and stakeholders are included?
3. **Establish the baseline:** Current volume, time, cost, error, quality, service, and financial performance.
4. **Map the process:** Suppliers, inputs, steps, decision points, handoffs, outputs, and customers.
5. **Segment the data:** Product, region, channel, team, customer, time, process step, or exception type.
6. **Identify root causes:** Separate symptoms from causes and test each hypothesis with evidence.
7. **Design options:** Compare impact, effort, cost, risk, implementation time, and stakeholder acceptance.
8. **Recommend:** State what should change, why, expected directional impact, assumptions, and risks.
9. **Implement:** Assign owners, milestones, resources, communications, training, and controls.
10. **Sustain:** Track KPIs, review performance, resolve exceptions, and standardize successful practices.

Useful opening: “Before analyzing the data, I would confirm the client’s definition of success, scope, baseline, constraints, and decision timeline.”

## Operational Excellence Fundamentals

### Lean

Lean focuses on customer value, flow, and removing waste. Common waste categories are defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilized talent, transportation, inventory, motion, and extra processing. Do not claim Lean project experience; show that you understand the logic and are ready to learn MNP’s methods.

### Six Sigma and DMAIC

Six Sigma focuses on reducing variation and defects using evidence. DMAIC means:

- **Define:** problem, customer need, scope, team, and goal.
- **Measure:** process, baseline, data definitions, and measurement reliability.
- **Analyze:** root causes and drivers of performance.
- **Improve:** design, pilot, and evaluate solutions.
- **Control:** owners, standard work, KPIs, review cadence, and response plans.

### Process map, SIPOC, and root cause

- **Process map:** steps, decisions, handoffs, delays, rework, and ownership.
- **SIPOC:** suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers; useful for scoping.
- **Five Whys:** iteratively tests why a problem occurs.
- **Fishbone:** organizes possible causes under categories such as people, process, technology, materials, measurement, and environment.

## KPI Design

A good KPI is connected to an objective, clearly defined, measurable from reliable data, owned by someone, reviewed at a specified cadence, and paired with a target and action threshold.

For each KPI state:

- business objective and definition;
- formula, numerator, denominator, and exclusions;
- source, owner, frequency, target, and threshold;
- leading or lagging status;
- segmentation and required response when off target.

Examples for a hypothetical process:

- cycle time, first-pass yield, defect/rework rate, on-time completion;
- throughput, capacity utilization, backlog age;
- cost per transaction, gross margin, revenue per unit;
- customer response time and service-level attainment;
- employee absence, turnover, productivity, or training completion.

Always ask whether a KPI could drive unintended behaviour. Never claim that Jainali implemented these KPIs for a client.

## Financial and Quantitative Thinking

Know these basic relationships:

- Revenue = price × volume.
- Gross profit = revenue − direct cost.
- Gross margin percentage = gross profit ÷ revenue.
- Contribution margin = revenue − variable costs.
- Productivity = output ÷ input.
- Capacity utilization = actual output ÷ practical capacity.
- Return on investment = benefit net of cost ÷ investment cost.
- Payback period = investment ÷ periodic cash benefit.

If numbers are missing, identify what data is required and use directional reasoning. Never fabricate a client saving. Say: “I would quantify the baseline and validate assumptions before estimating benefits.”

## Change Management and Implementation

A technically sound recommendation can fail if people do not understand, accept, or sustain it. A simple implementation plan should identify:

- affected stakeholder groups and impact;
- sponsor, process owner, and implementation owners;
- case for change and targeted communications;
- process, role, technology, policy, and training changes;
- pilot scope, feedback, milestones, dependencies, and risks;
- adoption and performance measures;
- escalation route and sustainment cadence.

Your truthful evidence is process-update coordination and Agile participation—not formal change-management ownership.

## Case Interview Practice

### Case 1: A distributor’s profitability is falling

Structure the problem into revenue and cost.

- Revenue: price, volume, product/customer/channel mix, retention, service levels.
- Cost: purchasing, labour, freight, inventory, rework, overtime, returns, overhead.
- Operations: throughput, bottlenecks, capacity, scheduling, stockouts, excess inventory.
- Analysis: compare periods and segments, identify the largest changes, trace root causes.
- Recommendation: prioritize a small number of high-impact actions, pilot, assign owners, and monitor margin plus operational KPIs.

### Case 2: A process has long cycle times

Map the current state; define start/end points; measure touch time versus wait time; segment delays by step and exception; examine approvals, batching, handoffs, rework, unclear roles, and system constraints; design and pilot a future state; track cycle time, first-pass yield, backlog, and service level.

### Case 3: Employee turnover is increasing

Clarify role, department, tenure, location, voluntary/involuntary, and critical-skill scope. Examine hiring cohorts, performance, compensation, attendance, manager/team, promotion, workload, and exit information. Combine quantitative patterns with interviews or surveys. Recommend only after validating drivers; measure retention and operational outcomes.
