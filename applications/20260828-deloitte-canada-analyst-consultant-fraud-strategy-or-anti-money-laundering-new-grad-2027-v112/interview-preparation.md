## Fraud Strategy Fundamentals

Fraud generally involves intentional deception for gain or to cause loss. A practical fraud-strategy lifecycle is:

**Understand threats → assess exposure → prevent → detect → investigate → respond/recover → learn and improve**

Useful control categories:

- **Preventive:** identity verification, access controls, segregation of duties, transaction limits, staff/customer education.
- **Detective:** rules, anomaly monitoring, reconciliations, exception reports, alerts, quality reviews.
- **Corrective:** investigation, account restriction, recovery, control remediation, disciplinary or legal escalation where appropriate.

Never recommend a control only because it catches more activity. Discuss customer friction, operating cost, false positives, data quality, privacy, explainability, and unintended consequences.

## Canadian AML Fundamentals

At a high level, anti-money laundering work helps organizations identify, assess, mitigate, monitor, and report money-laundering and terrorist-financing risk. Exact legal obligations depend on the organization and sector.

FINTRAC says a reporting entity's compliance program includes an appointed compliance officer, written policies and procedures, a documented risk assessment, ongoing training, and an effectiveness review at least every two years. Relevant operating activities can include client identification, beneficial-ownership and third-party determination, business-relationship monitoring, record keeping, and transaction reporting.

A simplified operational flow is:

**Onboarding and identity checks → customer/business risk assessment → ongoing monitoring → alert review → investigation and documentation → escalation/reporting when required → quality assurance and program improvement**

Important terms to understand:

- **KYC:** know-your-client processes, including identity and other applicable client information.
- **CDD:** customer due diligence proportionate to risk.
- **EDD:** enhanced measures for higher-risk situations, potentially including more information about source of funds, source of wealth, or transaction purpose.
- **Beneficial ownership:** the natural persons who ultimately own or control an entity.
- **Transaction monitoring:** reviewing activity for patterns or behaviour that may require investigation.
- **Alert vs. case:** an alert is a signal for review; a case is a structured investigation of relevant information.
- **STR:** a suspicious transaction report. Suspicion requires facts, context, and indicators—not merely a hunch—and sector-specific legal guidance must be followed.
- **False positive:** activity flagged for review that is ultimately not suspicious.
- **False negative:** genuinely concerning activity not detected by the control.

In an interview, do not state that unusual activity is automatically criminal. Say it may warrant investigation based on the customer, expected behaviour, transaction context, available indicators, and applicable requirements.

## Consulting Problem-Solving Framework

For ambiguous questions, speak in this order:

1. **Clarify the objective:** What decision is the client trying to make? What does success mean?
2. **Define scope and stakeholders:** Which product, channel, geography, customer group, systems, and teams are involved?
3. **Establish facts:** What qualitative and quantitative evidence is available? How reliable is it?
4. **Form hypotheses:** What could explain the issue? Separate symptoms from root causes.
5. **Analyze:** segment the data, compare time periods or cohorts, map the process, identify control and handoff gaps, and test assumptions.
6. **Develop options:** compare impact, feasibility, risk, cost, customer effect, and implementation effort.
7. **Recommend:** state the answer first, then the supporting evidence, risks, and assumptions.
8. **Implement and measure:** define owners, milestones, change activities, controls, and KPIs.

Use signposting: “I would structure this in three parts…” and end with a concise recommendation.

## Practice Case 1: Digital-Payment Fraud Increase

**Prompt:** A bank reports a 30% rise in digital-payment fraud. How would you approach it?

Strong structure:

- Confirm whether the 30% refers to fraud value, case volume, fraud rate, attempted fraud, or confirmed loss; establish the time period and denominator.
- Segment by product, channel, transaction type, customer group, geography, device, merchant, authentication method, and time.
- Verify data definitions and rule changes before treating the increase as real.
- Map the journey from onboarding and authentication through transaction authorization, alerting, investigation, and recovery.
- Compare recent patterns with the baseline and identify concentrated drivers or control failures.
- Interview fraud operations, product, data, technology, customer-service, risk, and compliance stakeholders.
- Develop options such as targeted rule tuning, step-up authentication, improved data capture, process redesign, customer education, or stronger investigation prioritization.
- Pilot changes and monitor fraud loss/rate, detection rate, false positives, customer declines, alert volume, investigation time, recovery, and complaints.

Example synthesis:

> I would first validate the metric and isolate where the increase is concentrated. I would then combine transaction segmentation with a process-and-control review to distinguish changing fraud behaviour from data, rule, or operating issues. My recommendation would prioritize targeted controls against the highest-risk segments, pilot them, and measure both fraud reduction and customer impact before scaling.

## Practice Case 2: Too Many AML Alerts

**Prompt:** An AML monitoring team has a growing alert backlog and a high false-positive rate. What would you recommend?

Strong structure:

- Clarify alert volume, backlog age, service levels, investigator capacity, case conversion, risk coverage, and recent rule/data changes.
- Segment alerts by scenario, risk tier, customer type, product, channel, outcome, and investigator.
- Check input-data completeness, duplicate alerts, threshold logic, scenario overlap, documentation quality, and handoffs.
- Review whether investigators have consistent guidance, training, tools, and access to contextual information.
- Consider risk-based prioritization, deduplication, workflow changes, richer context, targeted scenario tuning, QA, and staffing/training options.
- Do **not** simply raise thresholds to reduce volume; first assess whether the change could weaken risk coverage or create false negatives.
- Test changes in a controlled way, document rationale and approvals, and monitor backlog, alert-to-case conversion, review time, QA results, coverage, and escalations.

## Analytical Concepts to Defend

- **Segmentation:** break an overall result into meaningful groups to locate drivers.
- **Trend analysis:** compare performance over time while checking seasonality and definition changes.
- **Root-cause analysis:** distinguish the observed problem from the process, data, behaviour, or control factors producing it.
- **Data validation:** check completeness, uniqueness, consistency, valid values, reconciliation, and business-rule conformity.
- **Precision:** among flagged items, the share that is truly relevant; helpful when discussing false positives.
- **Recall/detection sensitivity:** among relevant items, the share detected; helpful when discussing false negatives.
- **Control effectiveness:** whether a control addresses the intended risk and operates consistently, without disproportionate cost or harm.

Possible KPIs include fraud loss and fraud rate, prevented loss, detection rate, false-positive rate, alert-to-case conversion, investigation cycle time, backlog age, recovery rate, control exceptions, QA error rate, and customer friction. Define the numerator, denominator, source, frequency, owner, and intended decision for every KPI.
