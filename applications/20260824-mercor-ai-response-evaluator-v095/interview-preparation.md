## The Evaluation Workflow

Use **TASK — EVIDENCE — JUDGMENT — RATIONALE — CHECK**.

### 1. Task

- What exactly did the user request?
- What constraints, format, audience, and scope were specified?
- Are there implicit requirements needed for a useful answer?

### 2. Evidence

- Which statements are factual claims?
- Which claims are supported by the supplied material?
- Which statements are inference, opinion, or assumption?
- Are quotations, dates, calculations, and definitions accurate?

### 3. Judgment

Apply only the provided rubric. Common dimensions include correctness, relevance, completeness, reasoning, instruction following, clarity, and safety.

### 4. Rationale

State the verdict, identify the decisive evidence, explain the impact, and distinguish major from minor issues.

### 5. Check

Re-read the prompt, rubric, response, and your rationale. Check consistency, omissions, unsupported claims, grammar, and whether your score matches your explanation.

## Critical Reading: What to Notice

- answer addresses a related question instead of the requested one;
- ambiguous pronouns, definitions, timeframes, or comparison bases;
- factual claims stated without support;
- conclusions stronger than the evidence permits;
- correlation described as causation;
- ignored exceptions, edge cases, or counterexamples;
- correct result reached through invalid reasoning;
- inconsistent standards applied to similar cases;
- omitted instruction, required format, or audience need;
- misleading precision, incorrect units, or denominator errors;
- verbose wording that obscures the decision;
- subtle contradiction between different parts of the response.

## Writing a Strong Rationale

A useful rationale has four parts:

1. **Verdict:** concise overall judgment.
2. **Evidence:** the exact claim, omission, or reasoning step that matters.
3. **Why it matters:** effect on correctness, completeness, or usefulness.
4. **Qualification:** acknowledge what is correct and whether the issue is major or minor.

Weak: “Response A is better because it is detailed.”

Strong structure: “Response A is stronger because it answers both requested components and supports the recommendation with the supplied figures. Response B omits the implementation constraint and assumes a cause not established by the data. Although B is concise and factually correct on the first component, the omission makes it incomplete.”

Do not reuse that wording in an assessment; practice producing your own rationales.

## Major Versus Minor Errors

A **major error** typically changes the conclusion, violates a central instruction, relies on fabricated evidence, omits a required part, or makes the response unsafe or unusable.

A **minor error** is localized and does not overturn the core answer, such as a small imprecision, limited explanation, or nonessential omission.

Do not grade based on how polished the prose sounds. A fluent response can contain a major logical or factual failure.

## Evidence and Inference

Label mentally:

- **Fact:** directly supported or independently verifiable under the task rules.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from facts.
- **Assumption:** accepted without evidence for the purpose of reasoning.
- **Opinion:** a preference or value judgment.
- **Unknown:** insufficient information.

Penalize an inference only when the response presents it with unjustified certainty or when the inference does not follow from the evidence.

## Consistent Rubric Application

Before rating multiple answers:

1. Translate each rubric criterion into an observable test.
2. Decide what counts as pass, partial, and fail.
3. Apply the same interpretation to every response.
4. Revisit earlier judgments if a later example exposes inconsistency.
5. Do not change standards because you agree with a conclusion or prefer a writing style.

If two criteria overlap, avoid penalizing the same issue twice unless the rubric explicitly requires separate scoring.

## Manual Practice Exercise 1

Prompt: “A store's monthly revenue increased from $200,000 to $230,000. Calculate the percentage increase and explain the result.”

Candidate response: “Revenue increased by 30%, meaning the store earned $30,000 more.”

Without using AI, write a verdict and rationale. Then check:

- Absolute increase is $30,000.
- Percentage increase is $30,000 divided by $200,000, or 15%.
- The response mixes a correct absolute increase with an incorrect percentage.
- The error is central because calculation is the main task.

## Manual Practice Exercise 2

Prompt: “Based only on the supplied survey, summarize the two most common barriers and state one limitation.”

Candidate response correctly gives the two barriers but adds: “These results prove the policy fails nationwide.”

Your rationale should identify that the requested summary is present, but the nationwide causal/generalized conclusion exceeds the supplied survey evidence. Decide severity based on the scoring rubric and whether the unsupported claim changes the overall usefulness.

## Manual Practice Exercise 3

Prompt requests exactly three bullets and no introductory text. A response gives an accurate paragraph followed by three bullets.

Separate content correctness from instruction following. The extra paragraph is a format violation even if its information is accurate. Judge severity according to how strict the rubric is about exact formatting.

## Likely Interview Questions

### “How do you identify a gap in reasoning?”

Explain that you map the conclusion back to its supporting premises, test whether each premise is supported, look for missing alternatives or assumptions, and ask whether the evidence is sufficient for the confidence level used.

### “How do you remain consistent?”

Describe converting rubric language into observable criteria, applying the same thresholds across examples, documenting decisive evidence, and conducting a final calibration check.

### “What if both answers have problems?”

Evaluate each independently, compare severity and task impact, identify any tie-breaking rubric criterion, and avoid declaring an answer good merely because the alternative is worse.

### “What if the rubric conflicts with your personal opinion?”

Apply the rubric. Flag a genuine ambiguity through the allowed escalation process; do not silently replace the project's standard with your preference.

### “Tell me about attention to detail.”

Use E-Commerce Sales Analytics: recognized-revenue logic excluded pending, refunded, and cancelled activity; relational constraints and repeatable checks protected the validity of later analysis. Do not invent an error rate or business outcome.

### “Tell me about written analysis.”

Use Online GST Filing Adoption Research: questionnaire, Excel analysis, identified adoption patterns and barriers, documented findings and recommendations. Do not invent sample size or source counts.

## 15-Minute Interview Strategy

- Listen to or read the complete question before answering.
- Take a few seconds to define the decision and relevant evidence.
- Lead with your conclusion.
- Give one concrete example.
- State limitations honestly.
- Keep the answer structured and concise.
- If you do not understand a term, ask for clarification instead of guessing.

## Final Self-Review Checklist

- Did I answer the exact question?
- Did I follow every instruction and criterion?
- Is my conclusion supported by specific evidence?
- Did I identify nuance rather than merely summarize?
- Did I distinguish fact from inference?
- Is my severity judgment proportionate?
- Would I apply the same standard to another response?
- Is the rationale clear, precise, and independently written?
