## Financial-statement foundation

### Income statement

- Revenue drivers: volume, price, mix, customers, products, regions, and acquisitions.
- Gross profit and margin.
- Operating expenses and operating leverage.
- Earnings before interest and taxes, taxes, and net income.
- Earnings per share and dilution.

### Balance sheet

- Cash, receivables, inventory where relevant, property and equipment, goodwill, and intangibles.
- Payables, deferred revenue, debt, lease obligations, and equity.
- Working-capital and capital-intensity implications.

### Cash flow statement

- Net income to operating cash flow reconciliation.
- Stock-based compensation and non-cash items.
- Working-capital movements.
- Capital expenditures, acquisitions, debt, buybacks, and dividends.
- Free cash flow definitions and limitations.

Understand the linkages before interviewing; do not claim professional modelling experience.

## Valuation foundation

### Discounted cash flow

1. Forecast operating drivers and free cash flow.
2. Estimate discount rate and terminal value.
3. Convert enterprise value to equity value.
4. Divide by diluted shares.
5. Test sensitivity to growth, margin, discount rate, and terminal assumptions.

### Trading comparables

- Select peers based on business model, growth, margin, geography, risk, and capital intensity.
- Common multiples include enterprise value to revenue, enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, price to earnings, and price to free cash flow.
- Explain why different companies deserve different multiples.

### Scenario analysis

- Bull, base, and bear assumptions should differ in coherent operating drivers, not arbitrary valuation multiples alone.
- State catalysts, probabilities where defensible, and thesis-breaking evidence.

## Information Technology sector questions

Depending on the company, examine:

- Recurring versus transactional revenue.
- Annual recurring revenue, bookings, backlog, and remaining performance obligations.
- Customer retention, expansion, concentration, and acquisition cost.
- Gross margin and research-and-development efficiency.
- Sales productivity and operating leverage.
- Cloud migration, usage, seat growth, pricing, and product mix.
- Stock-based compensation, dilution, capitalized development, and acquisition accounting.
- Foreign exchange, data-centre cost, hardware exposure, channel inventory, and working capital.
- AI monetization, compute cost, competitive differentiation, security, regulation, and implementation risk.

Use only metrics relevant to the specific company; do not force software metrics onto hardware or services businesses.

## Research workflow

1. Read public filings, earnings materials, transcripts, investor presentations, and material news.
2. Build an historical operating and financial model with cited source data.
3. Identify key drivers and compare results with expectations.
4. Research industry structure, customers, competitors, regulation, and technology shifts.
5. Develop estimates and scenarios with transparent assumptions.
6. Form a differentiated thesis, catalysts, risks, and valuation.
7. Write a concise note with facts separated from inference.
8. Update rapidly after earnings and track what changed versus the thesis.

## Writing sample expectations

A strong sample should demonstrate:

- A clear headline and investment-relevant question.
- Concise company and industry context.
- Evidence from authoritative public sources.
- Financial and operating analysis, not only narrative description.
- A differentiated conclusion, catalysts, risks, and valuation framework.
- Tables or charts with labelled sources and dates.
- Clear distinction between fact, estimate, and inference.
- Accurate citations, consistent units, and careful editing.

Do not submit a generic AI-generated company overview. Choose a public Information Technology company you can defend line by line, then build the sample from its filings and your own analysis.

## Stock-pitch structure

1. Recommendation and time horizon.
2. Current price and valuation date.
3. Two or three differentiated thesis points.
4. What consensus may be missing.
5. Earnings and cash-flow implications.
6. Catalysts and timing.
7. Valuation and scenario range.
8. Risks and what would change your view.

Never invent a target price or current valuation without current market data and a completed model.
