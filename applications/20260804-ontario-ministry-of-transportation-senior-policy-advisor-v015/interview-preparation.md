## Policy-Development Framework

1. Define the mandate, problem, affected users, current state, urgency, and decision required.
2. Establish outcomes such as mobility, access, safety, equity, productivity, affordability, sustainability, and reliability.
3. Build evidence from administrative data, travel demand, demographics, land use, infrastructure, costs, research, jurisdictional scans, and stakeholder input.
4. Develop options, including the status quo, and identify policy, legislative, regulatory, program, funding, information, partnership, pricing, or operational tools.
5. Assess economic, social, fiscal, environmental, legal, Indigenous, municipal, regional, equity, accessibility, privacy, and implementation impacts.
6. Consult stakeholders, test assumptions, document disagreements, and refine options.
7. Compare options against explicit criteria, explain trade-offs, identify risks/mitigations, and recommend a path.
8. Define governance, work plan, resources, milestones, communications, measures, evaluation, and corrective-action triggers.

State that this is a studied framework, not prior ownership of an Ontario policy file.

### How would you develop a congestion policy?

Define the corridor or system problem and outcomes; segment users, trip purposes, times, and modes; assess demand, capacity, reliability, incidents, land use, growth, freight, equity, and environmental effects; consult ministries, agencies, municipalities, Indigenous communities, operators, businesses, and users; compare infrastructure, transit, active transportation, demand-management, pricing, technology, land-use, and operational options; assess impacts; recommend a portfolio with measures and review points.

### How would you conduct an environmental or jurisdictional scan?

Define the question and comparison criteria; use authoritative legislation, strategies, evaluations, budgets, data, and research; choose comparable jurisdictions; document context and limitations; compare policy tools, governance, funding, outcomes, equity, and implementation; validate with experts; identify transferable lessons rather than copying another jurisdiction.

### How would you assess economic, social, and fiscal impacts?

- Economic: travel time, reliability, labour-market access, goods movement, productivity, business costs, development, and distributional effects.
- Social: accessibility, affordability, safety, health, connectivity, rural/northern access, and impacts on different populations.
- Fiscal: capital/operating costs, revenues, lifecycle costs, funding, affordability, risk, and opportunity cost.
- Document assumptions, ranges, sensitivity, uncertainty, and who bears costs or receives benefits.

### How would you build consensus?

Map interests, authority, impacts, and non-negotiables; establish shared outcomes and decision rules; provide common evidence; use accessible materials; hear concerns; identify agreement; develop mitigations; document unresolved issues; avoid promises outside authority; escalate decisions transparently.

### How would you brief senior management?

Lead with the decision and recommendation. Present the issue, evidence, stakeholder positions, options, impacts, risks, implementation, communications, and next steps. Separate facts from assumptions, state uncertainty, and prepare anticipated questions.

### Tell us about leading a project.

Do not claim project leadership. Use GST research or AYLA to show planning and coordination at the verified level: clarify the objective, break work into tasks, manage data and documentation, communicate progress, validate outputs, and complete findings. Explain how you would add governance, delegation, risks, and stakeholder management under experienced leadership.
