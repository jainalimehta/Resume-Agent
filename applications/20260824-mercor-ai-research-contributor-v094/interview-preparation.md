# Mercor AI Research Contributor — Interview and Assessment Guide

“I recently completed a Master of Business Analytics at Edith Cowan University in Australia and also hold an Integrated Master of Business Administration from Atmiya University in India. At AYLA Solutions, I gathered and documented business and reporting requirements, supported SQL extraction and validation, prepared advanced Excel reporting, and communicated progress through Agile planning and reviews. ## What Mercor Is Testing

The 15-minute AI interview and paid assessment will likely examine whether you can:

- understand an ambiguous question;
- break it into answerable parts;
- find or reason from relevant evidence;
- distinguish fact, inference, assumption, and uncertainty;
- write a direct, well-structured answer;
- notice contradictions and edge cases;
- apply instructions and evaluation criteria consistently;
- avoid unsupported claims.

Do not try to sound excessively technical. Precision, transparent reasoning, and instruction-following matter more than jargon.

## A Reliable Research Workflow

1. **Parse the task:** Identify the exact question, audience, output format, constraints, and success criteria.
2. **Define terms:** Clarify ambiguous words and the scope, geography, time period, and comparison basis.
3. **Plan subquestions:** List the minimum facts or analyses needed to reach an answer.
4. **Select sources:** Prefer primary and authoritative sources; use independent sources for corroboration when useful.
5. **Capture evidence:** Record the claim, source, date, and relevant context.
6. **Resolve conflicts:** Compare definitions, publication dates, methods, and source authority.
7. **Synthesize:** Lead with the answer, then explain supporting evidence and limitations.
8. **Verify:** Check names, dates, units, calculations, citations, completeness, and instruction compliance.

If browsing is not allowed, state assumptions and reason only from the information provided. Never imply you searched when you did not.

## Source Quality

Prioritize sources in this order when appropriate:

1. official documents, laws, standards, datasets, or company materials;
2. peer-reviewed research and recognized institutions;
3. reputable specialist publications;
4. established news organizations for reported events;
5. secondary summaries for discovery, not as the only support for important claims.

Assess authority, relevance, publication date, methodology, conflicts of interest, and whether the source actually supports the claim. A source can be reputable but still irrelevant to the precise question.

## How to Write a High-Quality Answer

Use this structure:

1. **Direct answer:** one or two sentences.
2. **Reasoning/evidence:** the key facts or analytical steps.
3. **Qualification:** assumptions, uncertainty, exceptions, or competing interpretations.
4. **Conclusion:** the decision or takeaway, without repeating the entire answer.

Keep facts and inference separate. Useful wording:

- “The source directly states…”
- “This suggests…, although it does not establish…”
- “Under the stated assumption…”
- “The available evidence is insufficient to determine…”

## Creating Strong Research Questions

A high-quality question should be clear, answerable, appropriately scoped, evidence-based, and difficult enough to require reasoning rather than simple recall.

Weak: “Is remote work good?”

Stronger: “Using the supplied productivity and retention data, which of three work-arrangement policies best meets the company’s stated objectives, and what trade-offs remain?”

Before submitting a question, check:

- Is there one defensible interpretation?
- Are all required terms and timeframes defined?
- Is sufficient information available?
- Does the question test reasoning rather than trivia?
- Can the answer be evaluated consistently?
- Does it accidentally depend on private, current, or unverifiable information?

## Creating Evaluation Materials

An evaluation rubric should define observable criteria rather than vague impressions.

Example dimensions:

- **Correctness:** factual and computational accuracy.
- **Completeness:** addresses every requested component.
- **Evidence use:** claims are supported and sources are represented accurately.
- **Reasoning:** conclusions follow from evidence without hidden leaps.
- **Instruction following:** format, scope, and constraints are satisfied.
- **Clarity:** concise, organized, and understandable.
- **Safety/integrity:** no fabrication, privacy breach, or unsupported certainty.

Define what a strong, partial, and failing response looks like. Avoid criteria such as “sounds intelligent” because they are not reproducible.

## Comparing Two Model Answers

Use this process:

1. Restate the task and mandatory constraints.
2. Independently identify the correct answer or required evidence.
3. Check each response for factual errors and unsupported claims.
4. Compare completeness, reasoning, relevance, and clarity.
5. Identify whether one answer reaches the right result for the wrong reason.
6. Choose the stronger response using the rubric, not writing style alone.
7. Explain the deciding error or advantage precisely.

Do not reward confident wording when the evidence is wrong.

## Common AI-Response Failures

- hallucinated facts, citations, quotations, or capabilities;
- answering a related but different question;
- treating correlation as causation;
- ignoring dates, geography, definitions, or units;
- performing correct arithmetic on the wrong inputs;
- stating an inference as a verified fact;
- missing exceptions or counterexamples;
- inconsistent criteria across similar responses;
- excessive verbosity that hides the answer;
- refusing a benign task or answering a task that should be limited for safety.

## A Simple Accuracy Checklist

Use **CLEAR**:

- **C — Constraints:** Did I follow every instruction and format requirement?
- **L — Logic:** Does each conclusion follow from the evidence?
- **E — Evidence:** Is every material claim supported or labelled as inference?
- **A — Accuracy:** Are facts, calculations, dates, names, and units correct?
- **R — Readability:** Is the answer direct, organized, concise, and complete?

## Quantitative Reasoning Reminders

- State the formula before calculating.
- Keep units consistent.
- Distinguish percentage change from percentage-point change.
- Check denominators and exclusions.
- Do a magnitude or reasonableness check.
- Do not infer causation from a trend alone.
- Explain what the calculation means for the question.

Example: If a rate moves from 20% to 25%, the increase is 5 percentage points and 25% relative to the original rate.

## Likely Interview Questions

### “Tell me about your research experience.”

Use Online GST Filing Adoption Research. Explain the research objective, Google Forms survey, Excel cleaning and analysis, patterns/barriers, written findings, and recommendations. Do not invent sample size, respondents, or statistical tests.

### “How do you handle conflicting sources?”

“I compare the exact claim, definitions, dates, methodology, and source authority. I give greater weight to primary or authoritative evidence, explain material disagreement, and avoid forcing a single conclusion when the available evidence remains uncertain.”

### “How do you know an AI answer is good?”

“I first define a task-specific rubric. I then check correctness, completeness, evidence, reasoning, instruction following, clarity, and unsupported certainty. A fluent answer is not high quality if it misstates evidence or reaches the right conclusion through invalid reasoning.”

### “Tell me about using AI.”

“My published analytics projects contain completed AI-assisted prompt and interpretation sections. I used validated SQL outputs as the grounding evidence, developed prompts for business interpretation, and documented insights and recommendations. I did not train or benchmark the underlying model, so I describe this as AI-assisted analysis rather than model-development experience.”

### “What would you do if you cannot verify an answer?”

State what is known, what is uncertain, and what evidence would resolve the gap. Do not guess. If the task requires a single answer, select only when the stated assumptions make it defensible.

### “Why Mercor?”

“The project fits the intersection of my research, analytics, and writing background. I am interested in learning rigorous model-evaluation work while contributing careful question design, evidence synthesis, validation, and concise communication in an independent remote setting.”

## Assessment-Time Strategy

- Read the complete instruction before responding.
- Identify required format, prohibitions, and scoring criteria.
- Budget time for a final verification pass.
- Answer the actual question before adding context.
- Use headings or bullets only when they improve evaluation.
- Show calculations when requested or when they make verification easier.
- Do not invent citations, access, credentials, or certainty.
- Keep notes free of confidential information from employers or clients.
- If tools or web access are allowed, use only permitted tools and cite accurately.

## Contractor and Availability Checks

Before accepting, personally confirm:

- availability for 10--20 hours per week and immediate-start expectations;
- comfort with independent-contractor status and variable project length;
- ability to receive weekly payments through Stripe or Wise;
- Canadian tax and record-keeping responsibilities;
- that no immigration restriction in the posting applies to your situation.

These were not added to the resume because they have not been verified.

## Final Accuracy Guardrails

- Do not claim AI-model training, benchmarking, annotation employment, rubric ownership, or work for an AI lab.
- Do not claim published interactive Power BI dashboards.
- Do not invent research sample size, sources, model names, accuracy improvements, assessment scores, or project outcomes.
- Say “AI-assisted analysis grounded in validated SQL outputs,” which is directly supported.
