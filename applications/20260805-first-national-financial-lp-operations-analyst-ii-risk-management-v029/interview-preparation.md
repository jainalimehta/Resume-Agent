## Risk Management and Internal Audit Fundamentals

Understand the distinction:

- **Risk Management** identifies, assesses, monitors, and responds to uncertainty that may affect objectives.
- **Internal Audit** provides independent assurance on governance, risk management, and controls.
- **Quality Assurance** checks whether work, decisions, or outputs meet defined standards and methodology.
- **A control** is an action or mechanism designed to prevent, detect, or correct an unwanted event.

Common control types:

- Preventive versus detective.
- Manual versus automated.
- Entity-level versus process-level.
- Key versus supporting controls.

Do not claim professional audit expertise. Present this as interview preparation and conceptual understanding.

## Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties reduces the risk that one person can initiate, approve, execute, and conceal an inappropriate transaction or change.

When reviewing potential conflicts:

1. Identify the sensitive process and incompatible activities.
2. Confirm roles, permissions, employment status, and actual system access.
3. Validate whether the conflict is real, temporary, approved, or a data-quality issue.
4. Review compensating controls where separation is impractical.
5. Document evidence, business rationale, risk, and exceptions.
6. Escalate anomalies through the approved process; do not accuse an individual of fraud based only on a system-access conflict.

Safe interview language:

> A segregation-of-duties exception is a risk indicator, not proof of fraud. I would validate the data and role definitions, preserve confidentiality, check for approved exceptions or compensating controls, and escalate the evidence according to policy.

## Fraud-Risk Review Framework

- Clarify the scenario, rule, population, time period, and risk definition.
- Confirm source systems, field definitions, completeness, and freshness.
- Identify unusual access, overrides, duplicate activity, timing, sequence, or incompatible duties.
- Compare activity with approved roles and expected process behaviour.
- Separate anomalies, errors, policy exceptions, and potentially suspicious activity.
- Document objective facts without inferring intent.
- Protect confidentiality and follow escalation and evidence-retention requirements.

Never investigate outside authorized scope or contact the subject directly unless the approved process requires it.

## Audit Sampling Fundamentals

Review at a conceptual level:

- Population, sampling frame, sampling unit, sample size, selection method, and exception.
- Random, systematic, stratified, risk-based, and judgmental sampling.
- Coverage, representativeness, bias, duplicate selection, exclusions, and reproducibility.
- Attribute testing: whether a defined control or condition is present.
- Sampling risk: the sample may not represent the population.

For a data-sample assignment process:

1. Confirm population criteria and period.
2. Validate completeness and remove only approved exclusions.
3. Apply the documented selection method reproducibly.
4. Check duplicates, conflicts, and assignment capacity.
5. Preserve selection logic, parameters, timestamps, and outputs.
6. Reconcile assigned samples to the generated population and sample counts.
7. Escalate anomalies rather than silently changing methodology.

Do not claim prior audit-sampling experience.

## Data Quality and Reconciliation

Check:

- Completeness: required records and fields are present.
- Accuracy: values reflect the authoritative source.
- Consistency: definitions and formats agree across systems.
- Validity: values follow allowed domains and business rules.
- Uniqueness: duplicate records are identified and resolved.
- Timeliness: data is current for the decision.
- Referential integrity: relationships point to valid records.

Reconciliation approach:

- Define source and target populations and grain.
- Compare record counts, totals, key distributions, missing matches, duplicates, and exceptions.
- Trace differences to timing, mapping, transformation, business rules, or source quality.
- Document unresolved items, owners, impact, and next action.

## Root-Cause Analysis

When a report or control result is wrong:

- Define the problem precisely: expected versus actual, scope, timing, and business impact.
- Stabilize the situation if an immediate control or reporting risk exists.
- Gather evidence across data, process, people, system, and policy.
- Use repeated questioning, cause-and-effect categories, or process tracing.
- Distinguish root cause, contributing factors, and symptoms.
- Test the proposed explanation against evidence.
- Recommend corrective and preventive actions with owners and monitoring.
- Validate after implementation and document lessons learned.

Do not claim professional ownership of formal root-cause investigations.

## Autonomy and Escalation

For unfamiliar risk-sensitive work:

- Confirm authority, methodology, and acceptance criteria before starting.
- Work independently within approved boundaries.
- Preserve reproducibility and evidence.
- Escalate anomalies based on impact, urgency, uncertainty, or policy.
- Provide facts, what was checked, likely impact, options, and recommended next step.
- Never suppress or quietly correct a material exception without documenting it.

Good answer:
