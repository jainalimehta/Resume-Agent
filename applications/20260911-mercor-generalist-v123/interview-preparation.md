## Core Evaluation Framework

Use **Purpose — Accuracy — Completeness — Relevance — Clarity — Presentation — Verdict**.

1. **Purpose:** What must the document accomplish, for whom, and under which constraints?
2. **Accuracy:** Are facts, calculations, reasoning, labels, and conclusions correct and internally consistent?
3. **Completeness:** Does it address every explicit requirement and necessary supporting element?
4. **Relevance:** Does each section help answer the task, or does it introduce distraction or unsupported material?
5. **Clarity:** Is the structure logical, concise, unambiguous, and suitable for the audience?
6. **Presentation:** Are layout, tables, charts, headings, terminology, and formatting consistent and usable?
7. **Verdict:** Apply the supplied rubric, identify decisive strengths and weaknesses, and explain the rating.

The project's rubric always overrides this general checklist.

## Reviewing a Word Document

Check:

- objective, audience, scope, tone, and requested format;
- factual support and whether conclusions follow from the evidence;
- missing requirements, assumptions, limitations, or next steps;
- contradictions, ambiguous wording, and inconsistent terminology;
- headings, sequence, paragraph structure, lists, and tables;
- names, dates, units, references, and cross-references;
- grammar, punctuation, unnecessary repetition, and unsupported certainty;
- whether formatting improves or obstructs comprehension.

A polished document may still be poor if it answers the wrong question or makes unsupported claims.

## Reviewing an Excel Workbook

Check:

- source, scope, time period, units, definitions, and intended output;
- missing values, duplicates, impossible values, and inconsistent categories;
- formula logic, ranges, copied formulas, and absolute/relative references;
- totals, percentages, denominators, rounding, and reconciliation;
- hard-coded values, hidden rows/columns, filters, and merged cells;
- consistency across sheets, tables, charts, and narrative findings;
- labels, date/currency/percentage formats, and chart units;
- whether an independent spot check reproduces important results.

Use control totals, row counts, reasonableness checks, and targeted recalculation. Do not claim macros, VBA, or advanced financial modelling.

## Reviewing a PowerPoint Deck

At the deck level, assess the objective, audience, storyline, supporting evidence, recommendation, risks, and next steps. At the slide level, ask whether the title communicates the takeaway, the content supports it, charts are labelled, numbers reconcile, text is readable, and visual choices are consistent.

Do not claim professional slide-review employment. Explain this as the method you would apply using verified PowerPoint proficiency and analytical judgment.

## Writing Structured Feedback

Use four parts:

1. **Verdict:** State the rating or overall judgment directly.
2. **Evidence:** Identify the specific text, calculation, omission, or formatting issue.
3. **Impact:** Explain why it affects correctness, usability, or compliance with the instructions.
4. **Correction:** State what should change when actionable feedback is requested.

Example:

> The document is mostly complete, but the recommendation is not fully supported. The analysis reports total orders while the conclusion refers to completed sales, and cancelled transactions are not excluded. This can overstate performance. Recalculate the metric using the approved completed-status definition and revise the conclusion accordingly.

## Comparing Two Outputs

1. Read the task, references, and rubric first.
2. List explicit and implicit requirements.
3. Evaluate each output independently before comparing them.
4. Separate major errors from minor issues.
5. Rank according to the highest-impact criteria, not polish or length alone.
6. Explain the decisive difference with specific evidence.

Both outputs may be weak. A response does not become acceptable merely because it is better than the alternative.

## Major Versus Minor Issues

**Major:** wrong conclusion, central calculation error, fabricated support, missing required component, serious instruction violation, or unusable format.

**Minor:** localized imprecision, limited explanation, or formatting inconsistency that does not change the main conclusion.

Severity depends on purpose. A small unit or date error can be major if it changes a financial or operational decision.

## Assessment-Day Checklist

- Read every instruction, reference, and output requirement before starting.
- Follow the supplied rubric rather than personal taste.
- Separate factual, reasoning, completeness, relevance, and presentation issues.
- Support every criticism with a specific observation.
- Verify calculations, dates, names, units, labels, and internal consistency.
- State uncertainty instead of guessing beyond the supplied information.
- Keep feedback concise, professional, and sufficiently evidenced.
- Reserve time to recheck the rating and required format.
- Use no external or AI tools unless the assessment expressly permits them.
