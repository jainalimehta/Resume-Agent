## M&A Lifecycle Fundamentals

A practical lifecycle is:

1. **Strategy and deal thesis:** Why transact? What strategic problem or opportunity does the deal address?
2. **Target screening and evaluation:** Which target fits the strategic and financial criteria?
3. **Due diligence:** Test the assumptions, identify risks, validate opportunities, and understand the target's operations, technology, people, customers, and finances.
4. **Deal planning and execution:** Determine structure, negotiation considerations, approvals, financing, and closing requirements.
5. **Pre-close readiness:** Define governance, integration/separation principles, Day 1 priorities, decision rights, and workplans while respecting legal constraints.
6. **Day 1:** Preserve business continuity, communicate clearly, establish control, and complete critical operational actions.
7. **Integration or separation:** Execute the target operating model, process/system/people changes, synergy initiatives, and transitional arrangements.
8. **Value realization and transformation:** Track benefits, resolve leakage, adjust the plan, and embed sustainable improvements.

Deloitte describes its M&A support as spanning strategy, deal execution, and post-close integration. This role's posting adds operational readiness, due diligence, pre- and post-close planning, separation, operating-model design, and transformation.

## Essential Concepts

### Deal thesis

The specific logic for why a transaction should create value—for example, revenue growth, market access, capability acquisition, cost reduction, scale, or portfolio reshaping. Diligence and integration should test and operationalize this thesis.

### Due diligence

A structured investigation of the target or transaction to validate assumptions, identify risks, understand performance, and inform price, structure, negotiation, and planning. Operational diligence may examine customers, products, processes, capacity, technology, suppliers, organization, and cost structure.

### Synergies

- **Cost synergies:** eliminating duplication, consolidating procurement, facilities, systems, or back-office work.
- **Revenue synergies:** cross-selling, new markets, pricing, channel expansion, or combined offerings.
- **Capital/working-capital opportunities:** inventory, receivables, payables, or asset optimization.

Every synergy should have a baseline, calculation logic, timing, one-time implementation cost, owner, dependencies, risk, confidence level, and tracking method. Avoid double counting.

### Value creation

Value creation is broader than synergy. It can include growth, margin improvement, productivity, pricing, customer retention, working capital, portfolio choices, operating-model redesign, and technology enablement.

### Target operating model

A target operating model explains how strategy will be executed through connected choices about capabilities, processes, people/organization, governance, service delivery, technology, data, and performance management. Do not describe it as merely an organization chart.

### Integration vs. separation

- **Integration:** combining selected capabilities, processes, systems, teams, and governance after a merger or acquisition.
- **Separation/carve-out:** disentangling a business so it can operate independently or under a new owner.
- **TSA:** a transitional service agreement under which the seller may temporarily provide services after close while standalone capabilities are built.

### Day 1

Day 1 focuses on legal close and immediate business continuity—not completing the entire integration. Critical topics may include governance, customer/employee communication, cash and controls, system access, regulatory obligations, and operating continuity.

## How to Structure an Ambiguous Case

Use this sequence:

1. Clarify the client's objective, transaction context, timeline, and definition of success.
2. Confirm scope: company, business unit, geography, function, systems, and stakeholders.
3. Restate the deal thesis and identify the major value drivers and risks.
4. Build hypotheses and determine what qualitative and quantitative evidence is needed.
5. Analyze the current state by function, process, data, organization, technology, and economics.
6. Define options and compare value, feasibility, risk, timing, cost, and stakeholder impact.
7. Recommend a practical path, including assumptions and trade-offs.
8. Translate it into owners, milestones, governance, communications, KPIs, and escalation.

Signpost clearly: “I would examine this in four areas: strategic rationale, operational readiness, people and technology dependencies, and value realization.”

## Practice Case 1: Post-Merger Integration

**Prompt:** A national retailer has acquired a smaller digital-first competitor. How would you plan the integration?

Strong approach:

- Clarify whether the goal is full integration, partial integration, or preservation of the target's distinct model.
- Restate the deal thesis: digital capability, customer growth, product expansion, cost efficiency, or another objective.
- Establish integration principles: what must combine, what should remain separate, and why.
- Set governance: executive sponsor, integration leader, functional workstreams, decision rights, issue escalation, and reporting cadence.
- Assess Day 1 needs across customers, employees, finance, technology, suppliers, data, controls, and communications.
- Map current-state differences and target-state choices for processes, organization, systems, and data.
- Build and validate synergy initiatives with owners, baselines, timing, costs, dependencies, risks, and benefits.
- Address culture, talent retention, customer disruption, data migration, technology compatibility, and change capacity.
- Track operational continuity, customer retention, employee attrition, milestone delivery, synergy realization, one-time cost, and unresolved risks.

Example synthesis:

> I would begin with the deal thesis and integration principles so the team does not destroy the digital capabilities it acquired. I would prioritize Day 1 continuity, establish cross-functional governance, and use current-state analysis to make deliberate target-operating-model choices. Synergies would be validated against baselines and implementation costs, with named owners and a dashboard tracking value, risk, customers, employees, and milestones.

## Practice Case 2: Synergies Are Behind Plan

**Prompt:** Six months after close, only 40% of planned cost synergies have been realized. What would you do?

- Validate the measurement: original baseline, realized vs. run-rate benefit, timing, one-time cost, double counting, and finance sign-off.
- Segment the gap by initiative, function, owner, and root cause.
- Test whether assumptions were wrong, dependencies were missed, decisions are delayed, capacity is constrained, or change resistance is affecting execution.
- Separate recoverable timing delays from permanently lost or overstated value.
- Reforecast initiatives and prioritize by value, feasibility, risk, and speed.
- Assign recovery actions, decision deadlines, owners, and escalation paths.
- Identify additional value-creation opportunities without masking the original shortfall.
- Report transparently: actual, forecast, variance, explanation, corrective action, risk, and decision required.

## Practice Case 3: Separation / Carve-Out

**Prompt:** A company is selling a business unit that depends on shared IT, finance, HR, and procurement services.

- Define the required standalone capabilities and the legal-close timeline.
- Map dependencies across systems, data, contracts, people, processes, facilities, suppliers, and controls.
- Decide which capabilities transfer, must be rebuilt, or need transitional services.
- Prioritize Day 1 continuity and identify stranded costs for the seller.
- Create TSA scope, service levels, costs, exit dates, owners, and migration milestones.
- Establish separation governance, issue/risk logs, decision rights, and readiness testing.
- Track standalone readiness, TSA exits, cost, operational incidents, talent retention, and unresolved dependencies.

## Analytical and Financial Concepts to Know

- Revenue, gross margin, operating expense, EBITDA, working capital, cash flow, and one-time implementation cost.
- Actual benefit vs. annualized run-rate benefit.
- Baseline, target, forecast, variance, timing, and confidence level.
- Sensitivity analysis: how the conclusion changes when major assumptions change.
- Segmentation: locating drivers by product, customer, geography, channel, function, or period.
- Scenario analysis: comparing base, upside, and downside cases.
- Data validation: completeness, consistency, reconciliation, business-rule accuracy, and source ownership.
- Qualitative synthesis: translating interviews and documents into themes, risks, hypotheses, and decisions.

Do not claim valuation-model or accounting expertise that is not on the resume. Demonstrate sound commercial logic and ask for missing inputs.

### What makes an integration successful?

Clear deal thesis, explicit integration principles, strong governance, early Day 1 planning, fact-based target-state decisions, talent and customer focus, disciplined change management, and rigorous value tracking.

### What can cause a deal to destroy value?

Weak strategic rationale, overestimated synergies, poor diligence, delayed decisions, unclear accountability, technology/data complexity, culture and talent loss, customer disruption, underestimated implementation cost, and weak execution discipline.

### How would you create an executive-ready slide?

Start with the answer as the title. Show only the evidence needed to support it. Make the implication explicit, identify the decision or action required, and place technical detail in backup. Check that the slide can be understood without narration.

### How do you work when information is incomplete?

Clarify the decision, separate known facts from assumptions, prioritize high-impact unknowns, triangulate sources, test hypotheses, document limitations, and update the recommendation as evidence improves.
