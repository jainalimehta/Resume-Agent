## The General Evaluation Method

Use **PURPOSE — CORRECTNESS — COMPLETENESS — CLARITY — PRESENTATION — VERDICT**.

1. **Purpose:** What is the artifact supposed to accomplish, for whom, and under what constraints?
2. **Correctness:** Are facts, calculations, reasoning, labels, and conclusions accurate?
3. **Completeness:** Are all requested sections, evidence, assumptions, and next steps present?
4. **Clarity:** Is the information understandable, concise, logically organized, and appropriate for the audience?
5. **Presentation:** Is formatting consistent, readable, and functionally useful?
6. **Verdict:** Apply the defined criteria, assign the rating, and explain the decisive strengths and weaknesses.

Always use the supplied rubric. This framework helps you inspect the work product; it does not replace project-specific instructions.

## Reviewing Documents

Check:

- purpose, audience, scope, and requested format;
- factual accuracy and support for material claims;
- logical flow from context to analysis to conclusion;
- missing sections, assumptions, limitations, or actions;
- internal contradictions and inconsistent terminology;
- headings, paragraph structure, lists, tables, and references;
- grammar, punctuation, tone, and unnecessary repetition;
- whether the conclusion is proportional to the evidence;
- dates, names, units, definitions, and cross-references;
- whether sensitive or irrelevant information is included.

A polished document can still fail if it answers the wrong question or lacks evidence.

## Reviewing Slides

Check both the overall story and each slide.

### Overall deck

- Is the objective and audience clear?
- Does the sequence build a coherent argument?
- Is the recommendation supported before it appears?
- Are assumptions, risks, and next steps included?
- Is the level of detail right for the audience?

### Individual slide

- Does the title state the takeaway rather than merely the topic?
- Does the body support the title?
- Are charts labelled with units, dates, sources, and legends?
- Are numbers consistent with other slides?
- Is text readable and free of clutter?
- Are layout, typography, colours, and terminology consistent?
- Does every visual have a purpose?

Do not claim professional slide-review experience in the interview. Explain this as the review method you would apply.

## Reviewing Spreadsheets

Check:

- source, scope, time period, units, and data definitions;
- missing values, duplicates, impossible values, and inconsistent categories;
- formula logic, ranges, absolute/relative references, and copied formulas;
- totals, subtotals, percentages, denominators, and rounding;
- filters, hidden rows/columns, merged cells, and hard-coded values;
- consistency between sheets, tables, charts, and narrative conclusions;
- date/currency/percentage formatting and clear labels;
- whether an independent recalculation reproduces key results;
- whether the workbook is usable by another reviewer.

Useful checks include control totals, row counts, reconciliation, reasonableness tests, and spot checks of high-impact formulas.

## Comparing and Ranking AI Outputs

1. Read the prompt, reference material, and rubric before reading the responses deeply.
2. Identify all explicit and implicit requirements.
3. Evaluate each response independently.
4. List major and minor issues by criterion.
5. Compare the severity and impact of errors.
6. Rank based on the rubric, not tone, length, or personal preference.
7. Write a rationale naming the decisive difference.

Do not assume one output is acceptable merely because it is better than the other. Both may fail the quality threshold.

## Writing Clear Feedback

Use four parts:

1. **Rating/verdict:** direct conclusion.
2. **Evidence:** exact issue or strength.
3. **Impact:** why it affects quality or usability.
4. **Correction:** what should change, when the task requests actionable feedback.

Example structure:

“Output B is stronger because its calculations reconcile with the supplied table and it addresses all three requested decisions. Output A omits the regional exception and uses total revenue instead of recognized revenue, which changes the conclusion. B still needs clearer source labels on the chart, but that formatting issue is less consequential than A's analytical error.”

Use your own wording during an assessment.

## Major and Minor Issues

**Major:** incorrect conclusion, fabricated evidence, central calculation error, missing required component, serious instruction violation, or unusable format.

**Minor:** localized imprecision, small formatting inconsistency, limited explanation, or nonessential omission that does not change the main result.

Calibrate severity to audience and purpose. A small label error may become major if it makes a financial chart misleading.

## Generalist Judgment Across Subjects

You do not need to know everything. When the subject is unfamiliar:

1. identify the task and required standard;
2. distinguish domain facts from general reasoning and presentation quality;
3. use only permitted references or tools;
4. state uncertainty rather than guessing;
5. evaluate what can be verified from the supplied material;
6. escalate genuine ambiguity through the defined process.

Strong generalists know the limits of their knowledge and still apply consistent quality standards.

## Likely Interview Questions

### “Why are you suited to a generalist role?”

Discuss the combination of business analytics and business administration, plus work across education, investment services, e-commerce, healthcare, workforce, banking, and small-business research. Do not call yourself an expert in each domain.

### “Tell me about catching or preventing an error.”

Use E-Commerce Sales Analytics. Explain that recognized revenue excluded pending, refunded, and cancelled activity; keys, uniqueness rules, and checks protected data integrity; repeatable validation supported consistent results. Do not invent a discovered production defect.

### “Tell me about reviewing a spreadsheet.”

Use GST research or Bank of Baroda: cleaning and organizing data, formulas, filtering, pivot tables, percentages, charts, trend analysis, and translating findings into recommendations. Do not invent workbook size or formula-error counts.

### “How would you rank two good responses?”

Return to the rubric, identify which response better satisfies the highest-impact requirements, examine factual and logical precision, and explain the deciding difference. If effectively tied, state that only if the rating system permits it.

### “How do you give critical feedback?”

Focus on the artifact, cite specific evidence, explain the consequence, acknowledge what works, and make the correction actionable. Do not soften a material failure into a vague suggestion.

### “What if the criteria are ambiguous?”

Document the ambiguity, use examples or precedent if provided, apply the narrowest consistent interpretation, and ask or escalate through the permitted channel. Do not silently invent a new standard.

## Manual Practice Set

### Document

Review a two-page report you previously wrote. In ten minutes, list one correctness issue, one completeness issue, one clarity issue, and one formatting issue. Then write a five-sentence evaluation.

### Slides

Choose a public presentation. For each slide, write the intended takeaway, whether the body supports it, and one improvement. Then rank the three weakest slides.

### Spreadsheet

Build a small table with revenue, cost, and margin. Intentionally insert one wrong range, one inconsistent unit, and one missing label. Reopen it later and test whether your checklist catches all three.

### Ranking

Write two short answers to the same prompt—one factually stronger but less polished, one polished but incomplete. Create a rubric and rank them consistently.

## Assessment-Day Checklist

- Read all instructions and tool rules before beginning.
- Confirm the required output format and rating scale.
- Separate content, reasoning, and presentation issues.
- Support every criticism with a specific observation.
- Check numbers, units, dates, names, formulas, and internal consistency.
- Keep feedback concise but sufficiently evidenced.
- Reserve time for a final rubric and formatting check.
- Do not use any unapproved AI or external tools.

## Accuracy Guardrails

- Do not claim prior AI-output evaluation, slide-review employment, independent-contractor history, or English proficiency that has not been verified.
- Do not invent evaluation scores, project metrics, document volumes, or business outcomes.
- Use only your verified experience examples and describe transferable methods as methods, not completed client engagements.
